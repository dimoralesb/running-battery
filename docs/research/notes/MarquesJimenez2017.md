# MarquesJimenez2017: Fatigue and recovery in soccer, a narrative review
Citation: Marqués-Jiménez D, Calleja-González J, Arratibel I, Delextrat A, Terrados N. Fatigue and recovery in soccer: evidence and challenges. Open Sports Sci J 2017;10(Suppl 1: M5):52–70. doi:10.2174/1875399X01710010052
Type: narrative review ("a comprehensive review of the scientific literature", p.52); no search strategy, no quality rating, no pooling.
Context: fatigue during and after matches, recovery between matches, and markers used to monitor them (performance, subjective, cognitive, biochemical). Mixes male and female, elite and sub-elite studies.
Evidence strength: low as a source of numbers. The publisher (Bentham Open) needs care: its journals were on Beall's list of potentially predatory publishers. Checked on Crossref 2026-10-17 (Bentham Science Publishers, vol 10, pp.52–70, published 2017-04-28, cited 67 times). Every match number is second-hand, and the originals are mostly already in our set (Bradley2009, Bradley2013, Mohr2003, Mohr2005, Carling2011). Useful for **framing** (fatigue has many causes, players pace, players differ), not for parameters.

## Definitions and thresholds
- Fatigue: "the inability to maintain a given exercise intensity or power output level", or more broadly "any reduction in physical or mental performance" (p.52).
- Recovery has three phases: immediate (within a bout), short-term (between bouts), training (between sessions or matches) (p.56).
- No speed thresholds of its own; the "high-intensity" numbers are Bradley2009's (>4.0 m/s [14.4 km/h]).

## Key numbers   (each with page/table/figure)
Second-hand; the original source is in brackets.
- Second half total distance up to 18% lower than the first, any position (p.53) [Bradley 2010, ref 20; not in our set].
- High-intensity running 20% lower in the last 15 min than the first 15, any position (p.53) [Bradley2009].
- Recovery time between very-high-intensity runs 72 s on average, +28% in the last 15 min (83 ± 26 vs 65 ± 20 s) (p.53) [Bradley2009, matches our §3.5].
- High-intensity running after the most intense 5 min is about 8% below average (p.53) [Bradley2013].
- Substitutes do about 15% more high-intensity running for the same period (p.55) [Bradley2013, Table IV].
- No drop in maximal sprinting speed within repeated-sprint bouts of up to five sprints in matches (p.53) [Schimpchen 2016, ref 24; not in our set].
- After a match: sprint about −3%, jump −4.4 ± 0.8% (p.53) [Rampinini 2011; Andersson 2008, elite women].
- Swedish players with low thigh glycogen ran 25% less (p.54) [ref 29, an old study].
- Fluid loss vs post-match sprint fatigue index r = 0.73 (p.54) [ref 38]; core temperature 39.0–39.5 °C in matches (p.54).
- 48–72 h to restore metabolic balance, muscle damage and anaerobic performance after a match (p.56).
- During fixture congestion, total and high-intensity distance were **not** affected by the short recovery; situational variables mattered more (p.56) [Rey 2010, ref 87; Carling 2015, ref 62]. Muscle-damage markers can stay raised for up to 72 h (p.54, p.59–60).

## Fig. 1 (p.60): which markers are useful
The symbols are images, so the .md loses them; read from the rendered PDF page. Columns: fatigue / muscle damage / recovery between sessions.
- Useful for **fatigue**: vertical jump, maximal voluntary strength, sprint, repeated-sprint ability, aerobic tests, subjective markers, cognitive markers, lactate, ammonia, urea.
- **Skill performance: "?" for all three**, i.e. not established as a fatigue marker. The text says players seem to keep their technical abilities during and after a match, so skills "may not be a reliable tool to detect fatigued players" (p.58).
- Hormones (testosterone, cortisol, T/C ratio) and muscle-damage markers (CK, LDH, myoglobin, IL-6, CRP): not useful for acute fatigue, useful for recovery between sessions.
- Every marker in the figure needs a test, a questionnaire or a blood sample. **None of them can be seen from tracking.**

## Main messages (with pages)
1. **Fatigue has many causes**: potassium build-up in the muscle for temporary fatigue, low muscle temperature at the start of the second half, glycogen depletion late on, plus dehydration, heat, muscle damage and central fatigue (p.53–54). Matches our §3.5 mechanisms [Mohr2005].
2. **Less running is not always fatigue.** "Professional soccer players regulate their physical efforts according to the specific demands of each game"; individual activity is highly variable and includes "elements of self-pacing" (p.55). Opponent, score, venue, formation and substitutes all change running (p.55). "It is complicated to evaluate the influence of fatigue on the physical performance of soccer players without considering ... situational variables" (p.55).
3. **Players differ.** The same match produces different responses in different players (training status, age, fibre type, p.56), and "the variability of the responses amongst players leads us to recommend an individual analysis" (p.61).
4. **There is no agreed way to measure it.** "Lack of consensus about the usefulness of tests used to monitor fatigue and recovery" (p.52, p.56); "no single biomarker has been validated for daily routine" (p.58). They recommend combining one performance, one subjective or cognitive, and one biochemical marker (p.61).

## Limitations
- Narrative review, no method for selecting or weighing studies.
- Copies primary numbers with errors. Example: Bradley2009's possession values are given as "148 ± 78 m vs 193 ± 9 m ... between the first 15 min and last 15-min period" (p.53). Bradley2009's abstract has 148 ± 78 (last 15) vs 193 ± **96** (first 15), so the order is reversed and the SD has lost a digit. Always cite the primary paper.
- Mixes women's, youth, semi-professional and lab studies with elite men's matches without separating them.
- 2017, and the match numbers it cites are from 2005–2013.

## Use in Running Battery
- **Simulator**: nothing new to calibrate. The numbers it repeats are already our targets from the primary papers (Bradley2009 −21% and +28%; Bradley2013 substitutes +15%).
- **Fatigue estimator**: supports the decision not to claim who is tired. Players respond differently to the same match (p.56, p.61), and even with lab tests and blood samples there is no agreed fatigue marker (p.56, p.58). A positions-only estimator can't do better than that, so "work that would leave a typical professional at X%" is the honest claim (PLAN decision 14).
- **Agents / claims**: secondary support for the context check (self-pacing, situational variables, p.55) and for blocking "missed the pass because he was tired" (skills preserved, p.58; Fig. 1 "?"). Secondary support for not modelling fatigue carried over from the previous match: running during congested fixtures was not affected (p.56); the muscle-damage markers that stay high for 72 h are invisible in tracking.
- **Overlay / UI**: none.

## Ideas or questions it raised
- Fig. 1 makes a point we can use in the README: every validated fatigue marker needs the player in a lab or a blood sample. Tracking only shows the work done, which is why we report work relative to a typical professional.
