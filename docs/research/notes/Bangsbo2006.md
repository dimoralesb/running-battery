# Bangsbo2006: Physical and metabolic demands of training and match-play (review)
Citation: Bangsbo J, Mohr M, Krustrup P (2006). Physical and metabolic demands of training and match-play in the elite football player. J Sports Sci 24(7):665–674. DOI: 10.1080/02640410500482529
Type: review (narrative, not systematic; mixes the authors' own studies and older literature)
Context: mostly elite/top-class male players (Danish and other European top-class; Mohr 2003 time-motion study by video; Krustrup 2006 muscle biopsies in a non-professional friendly match; Danish national team training before the 2002 World Cup for Table II; Malmö FF 1970s for glycogen). Tracking: mostly video time-motion analysis; heart rate monitors; core temperature; muscle biopsies. Seasons and n are not given for most numbers.
Evidence strength: medium. Widely cited summary by leading authors, but a narrative review: many values are rounded ("around 85%"), sources are 1970s–2006, and n and methods for each number must be looked up in the original papers.

## Definitions and thresholds
- No speed thresholds defined in this paper. "High-intensity running" and "sprinting" are used as in Mohr 2003 (not defined here).
- Heart-rate zones used in training data: 80–90%, 90–95%, 95–100% of HRmax (Table II, p. 672).
- Glycogen threshold mentioned: ≈ 200 mmol/kg dry weight is "required to maintain maximal glycolytic rate" (p. 669).

## Limitations
- Narrative review: no pooled analysis, numbers are rounded, sample sizes and methods are not given in the text.
- HR-based VO2 (~70%) is an indirect estimate; the authors themselves say match HR overestimates VO2.
- Muscle metabolite data (lactate, CP, pH, glycogen fibres) come mainly from one study (Krustrup 2006) in a non-professional friendly match.
- Distances come from older video time-motion analysis (Mohr 2003); not comparable with modern 10–25 Hz optical tracking thresholds.
- The fatigue mechanism is left open (glycogen in some fibres at the end; possibly K⁺ for temporary fatigue; not lactate, pH, CP or ATP).

## Use in Second Wind
- Simulator: (1) peak sprint speed ≈ 8.9 m/s (32 km/h) as a cap; (2) 150–250 intense actions per match; (3) temporary fatigue: high-intensity output drops below the game average for ~5 min after the most intense period, and repeated-sprint ability drops ~3% then recovers within the half; (4) end-of-match fatigue: lower high-intensity running in the final 15 min, ~3% slower sprints after the game; (5) large spread between players in the same role (2.0 vs 3.5 km high intensity). The hidden "true" fatigue can have a slow glycogen-like component (end of game) and a fast component (minutes, after intense spells).
- Fatigue estimator: no direct formula. Supports a two-timescale fatigue estimate (fast recovery + slow depletion).
- Agents / claims: allowed: "average heart rate in elite matches is around 85% of maximum, peaking near 98%" and "average oxygen use ≈ 70% of maximum" (review, older studies). Must not claim that lactate causes fatigue (the review argues against it). Must not present glycogen numbers as measured for our players.
- Overlay / UI: "estimated effort" could map our estimated intensity to a % HRmax scale anchored at 65% (floor during play), ~85% (match average) and ~98% (peak). It must be labelled as illustrative, since HR is not estimated from tracking in any of these papers.

## Ideas or questions it raised
- READING_LIST check: "average heart rate and energy demands" is half right. Mean (≈ 85%) and peak (≈ 98%) HR and ≈ 70% VO2max are given, but the review gives no match energy expenditure in kJ/kcal (the only energy values are training MJ/day in Table II). Activity-change counts and sprint counts are not in this paper (only 150–250 brief intense actions, tackles 3–27, jumps 1–36).
- Contradiction to watch: ≈ 70% VO2max (HR/temperature) is higher than what Osgnach2010's mean metabolic power implies (≈ 10.7 W/kg net ≈ 31 mL/kg/min, about 55–60% VO2max for a 55–60 mL/kg/min player; my calculation). Both are indirect. Buchheit2015's underestimation during recovery could explain part of the gap.
- For a fan "heart-rate-like" display, the 65% floor is useful: the estimated effort should not drop to resting values during short pauses.
