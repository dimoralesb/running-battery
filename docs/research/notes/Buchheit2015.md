# Buchheit2015: Is metabolic power, powerful? GPS metabolic power vs. measured VO2
Citation: Buchheit M, Manouvrier C, Cassirame J, Morin JB (2015). Monitoring locomotor load in soccer: is metabolic power, powerful? Int J Sports Med 36(14):1149–1155. DOI: 10.1055/s-0035-1555927
Type: lab/field validation study (soccer-specific circuit, not a match)
Context: France, elite academy, national level; 14 highly trained young players (15.4 ± 1.6 yr, 177.6 ± 6.3 cm, 68.5 ± 5.6 kg, VO2max 57.5 ± 5.6 mL/min/kg; 8 ± 2 h training/week). Two test days within 2 weeks. Tracking: 4-Hz GPS (VX Sport VX340a, Lower Hutt, NZ), same unit per player both days; criterion: portable gas analyser (Cortex MetaMax 3B), energy from VO2 and VCO2 with Brockway's equation. Season not stated.
Evidence strength: medium-low for our use. Only real criterion-measure test of the Osgnach model in soccer drills found here, but small n, youth players, 4 Hz GPS, a 4.5-min low-speed circuit with the ball, not match play.

## Definitions and thresholds
- P_VO2 (W/kg): metabolic power from net VO2 (and VCO2) via Brockway; computed separately for each 1-min effort and each 30-s recovery (p. 1151). It includes excess post-exercise O2 (EPOC) in the recovery phases. Anaerobic energy is not measured directly (RER 0.90 ± 0.07, all < 0.95, so mostly aerobic) (p. 1151, 1153).
- P_GPS (W/kg): Osgnach2010 equation applied to 4-Hz GPS speed and acceleration (each 0.25-s segment), via a custom Excel sheet (p. 1151). The KT and the smoothing used are not stated.
- Speed zones (p. 1151): > 7.2 km/h (2.0 m/s), > 14.4 km/h (4.0 m/s), > 19.8 km/h (5.5 m/s).
- Acceleration / deceleration zones: > 3 m/s² and < −3 m/s² (printed as "> −3" for both in Methods, a typo; Table 1 uses "> 3 m/s²").
- Power zones: 0–10, 10–20, > 20, 20–35, > 55 W/kg (Table 1).
- Effect-size scale (p. 1151): standardized difference > 0.2 small, > 0.6 moderate, > 1.2 large, > 2 very large. ICC: > 0.99 extremely high, 0.90–0.99 very high, 0.75–0.90 high, 0.50–0.75 moderate, 0.20–0.50 low, < 0.20 very low.

Protocol (p. 1150, Fig. 1): 10-min warm-up without ball; 2-min circuit at 6.5 km/h; 1-min rest; then 3 × 1-min circuit bouts at 6.5, 7.0 and 7.5 km/h (1.8, 1.9, 2.1 m/s), each followed by 30 s passive recovery; total 4 min 30 s. Circuit: 19-m lanes, slalom with ball, pass and receive from a rebound wall, shot, run without ball; pace set by audio beeps.

## Limitations
- Youth players (15 yr), 4-Hz GPS, n = 14, two trials, one short low-speed circuit (walking/jogging pace with the ball). Not a match, no high-speed running. The authors call for tests with adults, other tracking systems and higher-speed drills (p. 1154).
- The criterion measure also has limits: net VO2 misses anaerobic energy during efforts (partly picked up as EPOC in recovery), so part of the "recovery underestimation" is really effort energy paid back later. The 85% figure is therefore partly a timing mismatch, not only a model error.
- 4-Hz GPS acceleration data are noisy; the authors argue the effort-phase bias is not mainly due to sampling rate (Stevens found the same at 500 Hz → 10 Hz), but day-to-day CVs for acceleration distance (58–85%) probably are.
- Reasons offered for the effort bias (p. 1153), not tested: soccer accelerations are short and not mono-exponential; body lean differs from sprinting; dribbling and turning use non-locomotor muscles; individual running economy is ignored.
- Text inconsistencies: 29% (abstract) vs 23% (Results) effort underestimation; the CL of the −51.3% bias; Table 1 power rows do not add up.
- No per-activity breakdown (e.g., accelerations vs constant speed vs change of direction) was measured; only effort vs recovery phases.

## Use in Second Wind
- Simulator: none directly. Could justify adding a "hidden" energy cost in the simulator for actions the estimator cannot see (ball work, turns, EPOC after efforts), so the estimator is tested against a realistic gap.
- Fatigue estimator: (1) report drill/window averages of metabolic power, not "distance > 20 W/kg" (the authors' explicit recommendation, p. 1153: CV 73.6%, ICC 0.09 for > 20 W/kg distance vs CV 8.0% for average P_GPS); (2) expect a systematic underestimate: ~23–29% during movement, ~85% during standing recovery; (3) add an EPOC/recovery term if we want energy rather than locomotor load.
- Agents / claims: the verifier should block any claim of "X kcal burned" as a measurement and any claim based on small differences in "high metabolic power distance". Allowed: "locomotor metabolic power is an estimate; in a youth soccer drill it was about a quarter lower than measured oxygen use during efforts, with a typical error of ~14–20%".
- Overlay / UI: show the metabolic-power battery with a band. A defensible band from this paper: typical error ±14% (efforts) to ±20% (whole sequence), with a note that the estimate is more likely low than high. Prefer averages over 1–5 min windows rather than instantaneous zone counts.

## Ideas or questions it raised
- READING_LIST check: "GPS metabolic power underestimates real energy cost (~29% during efforts, ~85% in recovery)" is partly imprecise. 29% is the abstract number; the Results say 23 ± 10% and the mean bias is −24.3%. 85 ± 7% in recovery is correct. "Real energy cost" means net VO2-based power (aerobic + EPOC), not total energy including anaerobic. The setting is a 4.5-min youth circuit at 6.5–7.5 km/h with 4-Hz GPS, so the numbers do not transfer directly to 10 Hz optical tracking in Premier League matches.
- For our synthetic world the "truth" is our generator, so the Buchheit error sizes are only a guide for the UI band, not a property of our estimator. We can measure our own estimator's bias and TEE against hidden truth and report them in the same format (bias %, TEE %, r), which makes the validation look like a real sports-science validation.
- The finding that average P_GPS is reliable (CV 8%) but zone distances are not (CV 58–85%) supports using rolling averages (1/2/5 min) in the estimator, as already planned.
