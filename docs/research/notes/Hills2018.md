# Hills2018: Soccer substitutes: timing, running output, preparation (review)
Citation: Hills SP, Barwood MJ, Radcliffe JN, Cooke CB, Kilduff LP, Cook CJ, Russell M (2018). Profiling the responses of soccer substitutes: a review of current literature. Sports Medicine 48:2255–2269. DOI: 10.1007/s40279-018-0962-9
Type: review (narrative review with a structured search: PubMed, SPORTDiscus, Google Scholar, ScienceDirect, Feb 2018; 13 studies kept from 19 full texts; no meta-analysis, no quality scoring)
Context: 13 studies: 6 on timing of substitutions, 7 on match performance, 3 on psychology (some in more than one group) (p. 2257). Leagues: EPL, Serie A, La Liga, French Ligue 1, Brazilian Série A, US NCAA women (unlimited rolling subs). Tracking: multiple-camera optical (EPL, Ligue 1), video time-motion (Mohr 2003), 5 Hz GPS (NCAA), heart rate (Brazil), and official reports / ESPN data for timing studies (Tables 1–2, pp. 2258–2260).
Evidence strength: medium for "substitutes run more per minute than whole-match players in the same period" (several studies agree, including one large EPL study, n = 286 subs); low for "subs beat the players they replace" (mixed results); low for warm-up/physiology (mostly extrapolated from half-time and rugby research).

### Within-bout changes after entry (Sec. 3.2.4, p. 2264)
- Only one study: no significant differences between successive 5-min periods after entry, but a trend for TD and HIR to rise as the match goes on (Bradley 2014). Authors read this as a slow start (not fully warmed up) and/or pacing toward an end spurt.

### Warm-up and readiness (Sec. 1, p. 2256; Fig. 1; Sec. 3.2.4, pp. 2264–2265)
- Typical match day (Fig. 1, p. 2256; one team, empirical): pre-match warm-up from −45 to −20 min before kick-off; seated most of the 1st half; sporadic re-warm-ups; on-pitch activity at half-time; more frequent re-warm-ups in the 2nd half; example entry at 75 min (~90 min after kick-off counting half-time).
- Muscle temperature can fall by up to 2 °C in ~15 min of inactivity; r = 0.60 between the size of the drop and the fall in sprint performance over half-time (Mohr 2004) (p. 2264–2265).
- Rugby union: benefit of passive heat maintenance at half-time disappeared after the first two sprints of the 2nd half (Russell 2015) (p. 2265).
- No study has measured substitutes' physiology (temperature, glycogen, lactate) or post-match fatigue (p. 2266).

### Outcome links
- Ligue 1 club over 5 seasons (190 matches): in the title-winning season, +5 more matches won by goals from substitutes and +7 more matches won by goals at 75–90 min than in other seasons (Carling 2015; Table 2, p. 2260).
- 16–30 attacking plays and ~10 shots per goal (p. 2264, secondary).

### Psychology (Sec. 3.3, p. 2265; Table 3)
- Substitutes report mostly negative emotions (dissatisfaction, anxiety, reduced motivation to prepare, poor communication). Interview studies, n = 1 to 20. A routine warm-up helps keep task focus.

## Limitations
- Narrative review; no effect sizes are given by the review itself; few studies (7 on performance); most are small (n = 13–25 subs) except Bradley 2014 (EPL, 286 subs) and Bradley & Noakes 2013 (65).
- Very different speed thresholds and systems between studies (see table). Percentages cannot be pooled.
- Per-minute rates over short bouts (~15–25 min) are not directly comparable with rates over 75–90 min: shorter bouts give higher m/min for reasons of pacing and match tempo, not only "fresh legs" (authors, p. 2263).
- Selection: players picked as starters tend to be faster and stronger than non-starters (p. 2263), so sub vs whole-match comparisons mix fitness and freshness.
- Reasons for each substitution (tactical, injury, fatigue) are never linked to performance.
- NCAA women's data with unlimited interchanges are a different game.
- Figure 1 is one team's routine, not data.

## Use in Second Wind
- Simulator: substitution timing distribution: 1st sub mean ~57–58 min with a spike at half-time, 2nd ~71 min, 3rd ~81 min with a spike in 2nd-half stoppage; ~82% of teams make all three subs (old 3-sub rule; current EPL rules allow 5, so timing will differ). Make losing teams substitute earlier; make later subs more often attackers/midfielders (EPL). Sub output: +7–12% TD and +15–27% HIR per minute vs the same-period whole-match player; about +3% TD and +10% HIR vs the player replaced; no change in sprint distance; lower top speed than the replaced player; start lower and ramp up over the first 10–15 min (especially forwards). Midfielders give the biggest gain; full-backs no HIR gain.
- Fatigue estimator: a substitute should enter with a full battery but also with a "cold" penalty for the first minutes (muscle temperature). Their per-minute HIR is a useful "fresh" reference for the same team's tired players in the same phase.
- Agents / claims: the "fresh substitute makes the difference" story is supported for running volume ("covers ~10–25% more high-speed ground per minute than the player he replaced / than tired teammates"). Not supported: "the sub is faster" (replaced players reached higher max speeds), "the sub passes better" (no difference in pass completion), "subs always win games" (only one club, 5 seasons, outcome link). Also not supported: "came on and immediately at full intensity": evidence points to a ramp-up.
- Overlay / UI: show the substitute's battery entering at 100% with a short "warming up" shading for the first ~5–10 min; compare his live HIR/min with the player he replaced.

## Ideas or questions it raised
- READING_LIST.md says "Fresh substitutes. / Substitute output vs. players replaced." Correct theme, but the key nuance is missing: vs the players they replace the evidence is mixed (TD higher; HIR +10% in EPL but not in Ligue 1 forwards; FBs no gain), and subs do not exceed their OWN first-half intensity. The clearest result is subs vs whole-match players in the same period (+15–27% HIR per min). The review gives no effect sizes; the "fresh legs" effect sizes are in the primary studies (Bradley & Noakes 2013: ES 0.5–0.6 own same-period; ES 1.0–1.1 vs other players on the pitch).
- Story control idea: "fresh sub vs tired full-back" is the most defensible scenario: a fresh attacker (or midfielder) running at a full-back whose battery is low. The evidence is about volume and effort, so the agent should talk about "more runs" and "getting back", not about speed or skill.
- Gap: no study models a substitute's within-bout recovery after his own peak spell. Our simulator can treat it like any player.
- Question: modern 5-sub rules (EPL since 2022/23) change timing; the timing numbers here are from the 3-sub era (data to ~2015).
