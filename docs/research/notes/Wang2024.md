# Wang2024: Factors that affect short passing, LSPT studies (systematic review)
Citation: Wang B, Wan B, Chen S, Zhang Y, Bai X, Xiao W, Tang C, Long B (2024). A systematic review of the factors that affect soccer players' short-passing ability—based on the Loughborough Soccer Passing Test. BMC Sports Science, Medicine and Rehabilitation 16:96. https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-024-00880-y
Type: review (systematic, qualitative only; no meta-analysis)
Context: 33 studies that used the Loughborough Soccer Passing Test (LSPT); search to 10 July 2023 (Web of Science, PubMed, EBSCOhost). Ages 8 to 25.5 y; mostly male; levels from school/amateur to "professional" (as labelled by Wang). 9 studies on fatigue: 5 mental, 4 muscular. All lab/field tests; no tracking data; no match passing data.
Evidence strength: low. Small lab studies, vote counting only, almost no magnitudes. Several labelling errors (see Limitations). Useful only as a list of LSPT fatigue studies, not as a source of effect sizes.

## Definitions and thresholds
- LSPT (p. 2): 16 short passes against coloured target strips on benches around the player, as fast and accurately as possible. Three scores: execution time (time for 16 passes), penalty time (seconds added for errors: wrong/missed target, slow play), total time = execution + penalty. Lower time = better passing.
- Rampinini 2008 penalty rules (from the original, not Wang): +5 s missing target / wrong target, +3 s hitting target but not the coloured zone, +2 s ball touching a cone, etc.
- Outcome symbols (Table 2 note, p. 7): "#↑" = LSPT time significantly higher (worse) in experimental group vs control after the intervention; "##↑" = significant rise pre→post within group; "↔" = no significant difference; "↓" = lower time (better). "^2" = penalty time was the outcome; "^3" = number of targeting errors.
- PEDro score 0–10: 8–10 excellent, 5–7 good, 3–4 average, <3 poor (p. 3).

Magnitudes reported by Wang for fatigue: only Filipas 2021, U14 +7.4% and U16 +4.2% total LSPT time vs control, both not significant (p. 13). No other % or ES is given for fatigue studies. For the size of the match-fatigue effect, see Rampinini2008 note (penalties +43% after first half, +62% after second half).

Other numbers (not fatigue): verbal interaction at half-time −10.2% total LSPT time vs −4.2% with passive rest (Khalifa 2020, p. 14). Quality overall: 8 studies average (3–4), 18 good (5–7), 7 excellent (8–10) (p. 8).

## Limitations
- No meta-analysis, no pooled effect, no per-study magnitudes for fatigue except Filipas. Wang's conclusion "mental and muscular exhaustion have a significantly negative effect" is vote counting.
- The claim that "all five" mental fatigue studies showed significant harm (p. 11) is contradicted by its own Table 2 and text: Filipas found no significant effect in U14 and U16; Bian lists one arm worse and others better.
- The muscle-fatigue evidence is weaker than stated: Lyons 2006 found moderate fatigue improved LSPT time; Lyons 2021 compares elite vs sub-elite rather than fatigue vs rest; Wang admits two of four are "indirect" (p. 11).
- Labelling errors: Rampinini 2008 players called "professional" (they were 17.6-year-old juniors); the text on p. 13 swaps the protocols of Draganidis (resistance exercise) and Lyons 2021 (HIIT, female); p. 8 says four studies used Stroop and then lists five mental studies plus four muscle studies with mixed-up references.
- LSPT is a closed drill with no opponents (authors admit, p. 15). LSPT time partly measures dribbling and control, not only passing.
- Mental fatigue here means demanding computer tasks (Stroop, smartphone game) done before the test. It does not tell us anything about in-match mental fatigue.

## Use in Second Wind
- Simulator: do not use Wang for a pass-accuracy penalty size. If a lab-based upper bound is wanted, use Rampinini 2008 directly (LSPT penalties) and label it "lab test, junior players".
- Fatigue estimator: nothing. Physical fatigue from tracking does not cover mental fatigue, which is half of these studies.
- Agents / claims: allowed: "in passing drills, tired players make more targeting errors" (lab). Not allowed: "he misplaced the pass because he was tired" (match evidence absent here). If an agent mentions mental fatigue, the verifier should block it: we have no signal for it.
- Overlay / UI: none.

## Ideas or questions it raised
- READING_LIST claim check: "9 fatigue studies: muscular and mental exhaustion significantly worsen short passing" is Wang's own conclusion but overstated. Correct version: 9 lab studies (5 mental, 4 muscular); most find worse LSPT scores, but 2 mental-fatigue age groups (Filipas U14, U16) and one moderate-fatigue condition (Lyons 2006) did not, and one study (Lyons 2021) does not test fatigue directly. No pooled size is available. Use Rampinini 2008 for magnitude.
- The penalty-time (accuracy) component is more fatigue-sensitive than execution time (seen in Smith 2016, Bian 2022, Rampinini 2008). If we ever model a pass-quality penalty, it should be in precision, not in speed of play.
