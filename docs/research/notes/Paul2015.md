# Paul2015: Factors affecting match running (invited commentary)
Citation: Paul DJ, Bradley PS, Nassis GP (2015). Factors affecting match running performance of elite soccer players: shedding some light on the complexity. International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance 10(4):516–519. DOI: 10.1123/IJSPP.2015-0029
Type: review (invited commentary; short narrative opinion piece, 4 pages, no systematic search, no new data)
Context: no own data. Summarises other studies, mostly English Premier League (Prozone era, 2005–2013), plus Serie A, La Liga, female and international players. Tracking systems and Hz of the cited studies are not given.
Evidence strength: low as a source on its own (expert commentary). The numbers it quotes come from primary studies that should be cited directly. Co-author Bradley is also first author of Bradley2013, so it is not independent evidence for that paper.

## Definitions and thresholds
- No thresholds defined. "High-intensity" distances refer to whatever the cited primary studies used (for Bradley's EPL work usually ≥ 14.4 km/h = 4.0 m/s for HIR, or ≥ 19.8 km/h = 5.5 m/s for HSR; not stated here).
- Mental fatigue: a psychobiological state caused by long demanding cognitive activity, with feelings of tiredness and worse attention and decision making (p. 517).
- Pacing profiles (from Waldron & Highton 2014, p. 517): whole-match players "slow-positive" (gradual decline in total and high-intensity running); part-match players "all-out" or "reserve".

## Limitations
- Opinion piece. No search method, no quality appraisal, no pooled effects.
- Only a handful of numbers; no effect sizes.
- Opposition-quality categories in the cited studies are based on final league position, which the authors themselves call insensitive (a team can lose after playing well) (p. 517).
- Several numbers come from Bradley's own group, so the list is not a neutral sample of the literature.
- Mental fatigue evidence is from a lab running test, not matches.

## Use in Running Battery
- Simulator: confounders to include as seeded match context, so running changes are not all fatigue:
  - score line (leading team's CDs run less HIR; trailing team keeps HIR) — use Bradley2013 numbers;
  - formation × possession: in-possession HIR +30–40% in attacking shapes; out-of-possession HIR +20% in a 4-5-1; possession ~44% vs 50%;
  - home/away and opponent strength: direction only (home > away; more running vs strong opponents that are losing), no size given;
  - team quality: weaker teams run more at high intensity overall.
  Do not add heat or altitude effects from this paper (no match-running numbers).
- Fatigue estimator: running output is a noisy fatigue signal; physical variables are more stable match-to-match than technical ones (Bush 2015), so a player's own baseline over several matches is better than one match.
- Agents / claims: context checklist before an agent says "tired" (to be filled with numbers from Bradley2013 and other papers):
  1. Score line and time left (is the team protecting a lead, or chasing?).
  2. Possession share and phase (in vs out of possession): a player of a team with little ball may run more high-intensity without the ball.
  3. Formation / tactical change (e.g. switch to 4-5-1).
  4. Home or away.
  5. Opponent strength and opponent state.
  6. Ball-in-play time / stoppages in the window.
  7. Coach instruction or role change (e.g. after a substitution).
  8. Pacing: is total distance down but HIR kept? Then say "saving energy", not "tired".
  9. Fixture congestion / result already decided (qualitative only).
  10. Mental fatigue exists but we cannot measure it: never claim it.
  Allowed: "running less, but his team is 3–0 up and sitting deep". Not allowed: "mentally tired".
- Overlay / UI: a small "context" chip row (score, possession %, formation) beside the fatigue battery.

## Ideas or questions it raised
- READING_LIST.md says "Context factors. / Confounders for the simulator and agents." That is fair as a theme, but imprecise about what it delivers: this is a 4-page invited commentary with almost no numbers and no effect sizes; it does not cover altitude, and only mentions heat (via jump/sprint tests) and congestion (qualitatively). For usable effect sizes we need the primary papers: Bradley et al. 2011 (formation), Bradley et al. 2013 (possession), Castellano 2011 / Lago 2010 (location, opposition, match status), Trewin et al. 2017 systematic review (situational and environmental factors) and Julian2021 (congestion). Suggest adding Trewin 2017 (Sci Med Football 1(2):183–194) to the reading list; it is cited by Thoseby2022.
- The "effort/reward imbalance" idea (p. 517) could be a narrative line for agents ("the game is gone, the effort stops"), but it is a hypothesis; the Verifier should block it as a causal claim.
