# Dambroz2022: Physical fatigue and soccer performance (systematic review)
Citation: Dambroz F, Clemente FM, Teoldo I (2022). The effect of physical fatigue on the performance of soccer players: A systematic review. PLoS ONE 17(7): e0270099. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0270099
Type: review (systematic, qualitative only; no meta-analysis, no effect sizes)
Context: 12 experimental studies published 1996–2020 (search to 3 Oct 2020; PubMed, Web of Science, SPORTDiscus). Samples 9–42 players per study (paper says "10 to 30" on p. 10, but Table 5 lists 9 to 42). Levels: amateur, semi-professional, youth/academy, one U14 study. Fatigue induced almost entirely by lab or field protocols (treadmill, cycle ergometer, LIST, SAFT90, RCOD, soccer match simulation, circuits). Only one included study used real (friendly) matches: Rampinini 2008. No tracking systems; one study (Coutinho 2018) used GPS in small-sided games, Hz not given.
Evidence strength: low to medium. It is a systematic review, but the 12 studies are small, varied and mostly lab-based. The authors only give the direction of each result (arrows). They give no magnitudes and did not pool anything.

## Definitions and thresholds
- "Physical fatigue" = exposure to a fatiguing physical protocol, compared pre vs post (PICOS, Table 1, p. 3).
- Four outcome dimensions: cognitive (decision quality and time, reaction time, anticipation, visual search), technical (pass, kick/shot, dribble, interception), physical (sprint, distance at speed), tactical (team synchronization, dispersion) (p. 4).
- Quality score: 16-item adapted Law et al. form, score = items met / 16. ≤50% low, 51–75% good, >75% excellent (p. 4, Table 3).
- Symbols in Table 5: decrease / increase = significant; "maintenance" = not significant (p. 9).

How fatigue was induced (p. 10): treadmill (3 studies), cycle ergometer (2), soccer-specific field tests (LIST, SAFT90, RCOD), circuits, one 90-min simulation (Russell), one real-match design (Rampinini). The authors note that most protocols lack accelerations, decelerations, turns, jumps and ball actions (p. 11).

Verdicts as written by the authors
- Cognitive: "divergent", "inconclusive" (abstract; p. 12). Speed of response tends to improve after effort.
- Technical: "negative effects ... on pass, dribble and kick" (abstract; p. 13).
- Physical: sprint capacity and high-speed distance reduced (abstract; p. 15).
- Tactical: only one study; "lack of information" (abstract; p. 15).
- Key sentence (p. 11): no included study evaluated technical performance during the game.

## Limitations
- No effect sizes, no confidence intervals, no pooling. Direction only. It cannot give a "size of the passing penalty".
- Evidence per skill is 1–3 studies. Passing: 2 studies, of which one (Russell) found no loss of accuracy. Dribbling: 2 studies that disagree (Stone & Oliver worse, Russell unchanged). Shooting/kicking: 3 studies, all show some decline (precision or speed).
- Internal errors: (1) text p. 13 says Russell 2011 found lower pass accuracy, but their own Table 5 and the original paper show pass accuracy and success unchanged, only pass speed down; (2) p. 10 calls Russell's sample "professional"; they were youth academy players (Table 5: "15 young soccer players"); (3) PRISMA numbers do not add up (657 − 624 = 33, yet 146 full texts read; 146 − 21 = 125, not 12); (4) the paper says "qualitative and quantitative synthesis" but has no quantitative synthesis.
- Mixed populations (U14 to adult, recreational to semi-pro); almost no elite players. Lab tasks with low ecological validity (authors say so, pp. 11–12, 15).
- Carling 2011 (match study) is cited (ref 59) but not included, so the claim "no study in game situations" reflects their inclusion rules, not the full literature.

## Use in Running Battery
- Simulator: no numbers to take from here. Use as a guardrail: do not make the simulator's decision quality get worse with fatigue. Skill penalties should be small and mostly on kick speed/precision, not on choices.
- Fatigue estimator: nothing direct. It supports sprint capacity as the physical marker that fatigue changes (sprint down in 2/2 studies that measured it).
- Agents / claims: allowed: "in lab tests, tired players shoot less accurately and kick the ball slower." Not allowed: "fatigue made him choose the wrong pass" (cognitive evidence inconclusive; decision quality kept in 3 of 4 decision studies). Not allowed: any tactical claim about one player (1 study, U14, team-level only).
- Overlay / UI: README sentence (corrected): "A 2022 systematic review of 12 small, mostly lab-based studies found that physical fatigue lowers sprint ability and some technical skills (mainly shooting precision and kick speed). Effects on decisions were inconsistent, and tactical effects are almost unstudied."

## Ideas or questions it raised
- READING_LIST claim check: "12 studies" correct. "Harms passing, dribbling and kicking" is the authors' wording, but it is an overstatement of their own data: passing rests on 1 clear study (Rampinini) plus 1 where only pass speed fell (Russell); dribbling is 1 vs 1. "Decisions inconclusive" correct. "Tactics barely studied" correct (1 study, U14, 10 players, small-sided games).
- Coutinho 2018 (fatigued teams become more compact) could inspire a team-level overlay, but it is one youth SSG study. Use only as a hypothesis, not as a claim.
- The suggested index "passes / ball contacts" (p. 14) is easy to compute in our synthetic events; could be a neutral "tempo" stat, not a fatigue claim.
